How it works

In 2008 I wrote a converter in JavaScript from WGS84 coordinates to UTM on the ED50 datum, and described it in an article for Dev, an Italian programming magazine (the article, in Italian). This page sets out the formulas the code implements, with the fixes I made when I restored it in 2026, and the Gauss-Boaga grid of Italian maps, which I added then.

The problem

A GPS receiver, Google Maps or OpenStreetMap give a position as latitude, longitude and height on WGS84. Most official European maps drawn before 1984 use UTM coordinates on ED50, in metres. Two things separate them.

The datum is different. Each datum approximates the Earth with its own ellipsoid, of a different size and in a different position, so the same point has different latitude and longitude on the two. Around Trieste the ED50 latitude is 3.2″ larger and the longitude 3.4″ larger, which is about 98 m north and 74 m east.

The projection is different. UTM divides the Earth into 60 zones 6° wide and draws each one on a plane with the Gauss (transverse Mercator) projection, so that positions are easting and northing in metres.

So the conversion has three steps:

  1. move the point from the WGS84 ellipsoid to the ED50 ellipsoid, with the Molodensky formulas;
  2. project it onto the plane of its UTM zone, with the Gauss formulas;
  3. apply the UTM scale factor and false origin.

Notation: on this page \(\varphi\) is the latitude and \(\lambda\) the longitude, as usual. The 2008 code calls them the other way round: LAMBDA is the latitude and PHI the longitude. Angles are in radians.

The ellipsoids

An ellipsoid of revolution is defined by its semi-major axis \(a\) and its flattening \(f\). From them come the first eccentricity and the two radii of curvature at latitude \(\varphi\): \(M\) along the meridian and \(N\) across it, in the prime vertical. In the code they are RHO and N_G (the gran normale).

$$e^2 = 2f - f^2 \qquad M(\varphi) = \frac{a\,(1-e^2)}{\left(1-e^2\sin^2\varphi\right)^{3/2}} \qquad N(\varphi) = \frac{a}{\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2\varphi}}$$

WGS84WGS846 378 137 m298.257223563
ED50International 1924 (Hayford)6 378 388 m297

The 2008 code had 298.257222101 for WGS84, which is the flattening of GRS80, a slightly different ellipsoid. The semi-minor axes differ by 0.1 mm, so it made no visible difference, but it is fixed.

Step 1: Molodensky, from WGS84 to ED50

Moving a point between datums exactly means converting its latitude, longitude and height to geocentric \(X, Y, Z\), shifting (and possibly rotating and scaling) those, and converting back. The Molodensky formulas do it in one step: they give the changes \(\Delta\varphi\), \(\Delta\lambda\), \(\Delta h\) directly, to first order in the small differences between the datums.

The datum differences are the shift of the origin \(\Delta X, \Delta Y, \Delta Z\), small rotations \(\varepsilon_x, \varepsilon_y, \varepsilon_z\), a scale change \(\Delta K\), and the differences of the ellipsoids, \(\Delta a = a_{\text{WGS84}} - a_{\text{ED50}}\) and \(\Delta f = f_{\text{WGS84}} - f_{\text{ED50}}\). For ED50 the code uses the mean shift for Europe (EPSG:1133), \(\Delta X = -87\) m, \(\Delta Y = -98\) m, \(\Delta Z = -121\) m, with no rotation or scale change. These are the ED50 origin values; going from WGS84 to ED50 the point moves by the opposite, \(+87, +98, +121\) m. \(a, f, e^2, M, N\) are those of WGS84 at the starting point.

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta\varphi = \frac{1}{M+h}\Big[\,&\sin\varphi\cos\lambda\,\Delta X + \sin\varphi\sin\lambda\,\Delta Y - \cos\varphi\,\Delta Z + \Big(\frac{a^2}{N}+h\Big)\big(\sin\lambda\,\varepsilon_x - \cos\lambda\,\varepsilon_y\big) \\ &+ e^2 N \sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\Big(\Delta K - \frac{\Delta a}{a}\Big) - \big(M + (1-e^2)N\big)\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\frac{\Delta f}{1-f}\,\Big] \end{aligned}$$ $$\Delta\lambda = \frac{\sin\lambda\,\Delta X - \cos\lambda\,\Delta Y}{(N+h)\cos\varphi} - \frac{(1-e^2)N + h}{N+h}\,\tan\varphi\,\big(\cos\lambda\,\varepsilon_x + \sin\lambda\,\varepsilon_y\big) + \varepsilon_z$$ $$\begin{aligned} \Delta h = &-\cos\varphi\cos\lambda\,\Delta X - \cos\varphi\sin\lambda\,\Delta Y - \sin\varphi\,\Delta Z + e^2 N\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\big(\sin\lambda\,\varepsilon_x - \cos\lambda\,\varepsilon_y\big) \\ &- \Big(\frac{a^2}{N}+h\Big)\Delta K + \frac{a}{N}\,\Delta a - (1-e^2)\,N\sin^2\varphi\,\frac{\Delta f}{1-f} \end{aligned}$$

The ED50 coordinates are then \(\varphi + \Delta\varphi\), \(\lambda + \Delta\lambda\), \(h + \Delta h\). In the code this is molodenskij(). The 2008 version had two bugs in the scale change \(\Delta K\): it took it from the destination datum twice, so it was always 0, and in \(\Delta\varphi\) it had the wrong sign. ED50 has no scale change, so neither showed. Roma 40 has one, and the second bug put its northings 0.49 m off until I fixed it.

Step 2: Gauss, onto the plane of the UTM zone

The zone \(z\) comes from the longitude, and the projection works with the longitude \(\ell\) measured from the zone's central meridian \(\lambda_0\):

$$z = \left\lfloor \frac{\lambda + 180^\circ}{6^\circ} \right\rfloor + 1 \qquad \lambda_0 = 6^\circ z - 183^\circ \qquad \ell = \lambda - \lambda_0$$

Along the central meridian the projection keeps distances, so the northing starts from the length of the meridian arc from the equator to \(\varphi\), the integral of \(M\):

$$B(\varphi) = \int_0^{\varphi} M(\psi)\,d\psi$$

There are closed-form series for \(B\), but in 2008 I integrated it numerically with the trapezoidal rule. I found that 10 000 steps were enough: more steps did not change the first nine significant digits.

$$B(\varphi) \approx \frac{\varphi}{n}\left[\frac{M(0) + M(\varphi)}{2} + \sum_{k=1}^{n-1} M\!\left(\frac{k\,\varphi}{n}\right)\right] \qquad n = 10\,000$$

Away from the central meridian the Gauss formulas add a series in powers of \(\ell\). With \(t = \tan\varphi\), the second eccentricity \(e'^2\) and \(\eta^2 = e'^2\cos^2\varphi\):

$$e'^2 = \frac{e^2}{1-e^2}$$ $$\begin{aligned} x &= B(\varphi) + \frac{N}{2}\sin\varphi\cos\varphi\,\ell^2 + \frac{N}{24}\sin\varphi\cos^3\varphi\,\big(5 - t^2 + 9\eta^2 + 4\eta^4\big)\,\ell^4 \\ &\quad + \frac{N}{720}\sin\varphi\cos^5\varphi\,\big(61 - 58t^2 + t^4 + 270\eta^2 - 330\,\eta^2 t^2\big)\,\ell^6 \\[4pt] y &= N\cos\varphi\,\ell + \frac{N}{6}\cos^3\varphi\,\big(1 - t^2 + \eta^2\big)\,\ell^3 + \frac{N}{120}\cos^5\varphi\,\big(5 - 18t^2 + t^4 + 14\eta^2 - 58\,\eta^2 t^2\big)\,\ell^5 \end{aligned}$$

\(x\) points north and \(y\) east, and here \(a, e^2, M, N\) are those of ED50. In the code this is gauss(). The 2008 version computed \(e'^2\) as \(e^4/(1-e^2)\) instead of \(e^2/(1-e^2)\). Since \(\eta^2\) only appears in small correction terms, the error was at most about 14 cm near the edge of a zone.

Step 3: UTM scale factor and false origin

UTM scales the plane by \(k_0 = 0.9996\). The scale is then 0.9996 on the central meridian and exactly 1 on two lines about 180 km either side, so the scale error stays within about 1 in 1 000 across the zone instead of only growing towards the edges. UTM also adds a false origin so that coordinates are never negative: 500 km east, and 10 000 km north in the southern hemisphere.

$$\text{East} = k_0\,y + 500\,000\ \text{m} \qquad \text{North} = \begin{cases} k_0\,x & \varphi \ge 0 \\ k_0\,x + 10\,000\,000\ \text{m} & \varphi < 0 \end{cases}$$

The 2008 code left out the southern false northing, so points south of the equator had negative northings. That is the one fix in 2026 that changed results by more than a few centimetres.

Gauss-Boaga on Roma 40

Italian maps use their own grid. The IGM national maps and the regional technical maps (CTR) give positions in Gauss-Boaga coordinates, on the Roma 40 datum: the International 1924 ellipsoid, oriented at Monte Mario in Rome. In 2008 a reader of my blog asked for it, and I suggested switching the commented-out ITA40 datum in the code. That would not have worked: its flattening was given as \(e^2\), and its rotations and scale were in arc seconds and parts per million where the code expects radians and a ratio. In 2010 and 2012 a surveyor on Isola del Giglio asked me to convert positions for exactly these maps. In 2026 I added it properly.

It uses the same two steps. Step 1 is the Molodensky formulas with the Roma 40 parameters for mainland Italy (EPSG:1660), the official national set:

Shift \(\Delta X, \Delta Y, \Delta Z\)−104.1 m, −49.1 m, −9.9 m
Rotations \(\varepsilon_x, \varepsilon_y, \varepsilon_z\)−0.971″, 2.917″, −0.714″
Scale change \(\Delta K\)−11.68 ppm
EllipsoidInternational 1924: \(a\) = 6 378 388 m, \(1/f\) = 297

EPSG publishes the rotations as 0.971″, −2.917″, 0.714″ in the position vector convention; the formulas above use the coordinate frame convention, which has the opposite signs. In the code the rotations are converted to radians and the scale change to a ratio, \(-11.68 \times 10^{-6}\).

Step 2 is the same Gauss formulas, with Italy's two zones in place of the UTM ones, and no false northing:

ZoneLongitudes\(\lambda_0\)False easting
Ovest (West)west of 12° E9° E1 500 000 m
Est (East)12° E and east15° E2 520 000 m
$$\text{East} = k_0\,y + \begin{cases} 1\,500\,000\ \text{m} & \text{Ovest} \\ 2\,520\,000\ \text{m} & \text{Est} \end{cases} \qquad \text{North} = k_0\,x \qquad k_0 = 0.9996$$

The central meridians are those of UTM zones 32 and 33, but the coordinates are on another ellipsoid position, so they differ: for the Isola del Giglio point of 2010 the Gauss-Boaga northing is 182 m smaller than the UTM ED50 one (4 690 995 m against 4 691 177 m), and the easting is 55 m smaller once the different false eastings are taken off. Southern Puglia, east of 18° E, stays in the East zone.

Accuracy

I checked the restored code two ways. The first is an exact reference of my own: the geocentric shift, then the Krüger series for the transverse Mercator projection, which is accurate to well under a millimetre. At test points from Lisbon to Helsinki, and in the southern hemisphere, the two agree within 1 cm. The second is PROJ, the library behind most GIS software, with the EPSG transformations: at 18 points in Italy, both grids agree within 3 mm. The tests are in the repository.

That is the accuracy of the arithmetic, not of the datum. A single shift for all of Europe is good to a few metres, and EPSG gives the Roma 40 parameters an accuracy of about 4 m on the mainland; Sardinia and Sicily have their own sets. National mapping agencies publish regional parameters and grids for better results; in Italy, the IGM grids give sub-metre accuracy.

In 2008 a conversion took a few tenths of a second, which I considered fine for real-time use. I tested it in Internet Explorer 5 to 7, Firefox 1.5 to 3, Opera 9.5 and Safari 3. Today it takes about 3 ms, nearly all of it in the 10 000-step integral.
